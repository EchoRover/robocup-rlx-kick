Teaching a robot to kick and get up

Evan Tobias · RoboCup 3D, Booster T1 robot · full details and code

The idea in one paragraph

Nobody tells the robot how to kick. We put it in a simulated world, let it try millions of times, and give it points when the ball goes fast toward the goal and it doesn't fall. Bit by bit its "brain" (a small neural network) changes to do more of what earned points. That is reinforcement learning. It's how you'd learn a video game with no tutorial: try, see the score, repeat.

The words, simply

Policy
The robot's brain. It looks at what the robot senses right now and decides how to move every joint. 50 times a second.
What it senses
91 numbers: how bent each of its 23 joints is, how fast each is moving, which way it's tilting, and where the ball is compared to its body.
What it outputs
23 numbers, one per joint: "move this joint to this angle". A simple controller (like a spring) then pulls each joint toward that angle. That spring is the PD controller.
Reward
The points. Kick: points for ball speed toward the goal, for staying upright, for not falling. Minus points for jerky moves.
PPO
The learning rule. After a batch of tries: "that move went better than expected, do it more; that one went worse, do it less." But only a small change each time, so it doesn't forget what it already learned.
GRU
Short-term memory inside the brain. One snapshot doesn't tell you if the ball is moving or where you are in the swing. Memory of the last moments does.
Critic
A coach that only exists during practice. It guesses how good each moment is, so the learning rule knows what "better than expected" means. It's allowed extra information the robot won't have in a match.
Curriculum
Easy first, harder later. Like school: addition before algebra. The robot first practises standing still with the ball at its feet. As it succeeds, the start gets messier: tilted, joints bent, already moving.
Simulator vs server
The simulator is the practice pitch where it trains (RL-X, thousands of robots at once on a GPU). The server is the official match stadium. Both are physics engines, but set up a bit differently.
Reward hacking
The robot finds a cheat that scores points without doing the real task.
Local optimum
"Good enough" trap: the robot finds something that scores okay and stops looking for the better thing.
Is this standard?
Yes. This exact recipe (PPO, a memory network, joint-angle targets, a coach, easy-to-hard practice) is how most walking robots are trained today.

Kick: what I tried, and why each one failed

Step 1 · Training on normal computers

Way too slow: about 500 practice steps per second.

Why: the tools are built for graphics cards (GPUs), not normal processors.

So: moved to a big GPU. 4,096 robots practising at once, 75,000 steps per second.

Step 2 · Kick v1: the robot cheated

The score went up, but it wasn't kicking.

Why: it learned to stomp the ball into the floor until the physics glitched and the ball "flew" at crazy speed. Points for ball speed, so the cheat paid. That's reward hacking.

So: stop the try when physics glitches, and cap the points.

Step 3 · Kick v2: a real kick

Leans, swings, ball flies about 28 m. 64 out of 64 tries.

But if it starts a little tilted or moving, it falls 45 out of 64 times.

Why: it never practised messy starts. The easy-to-hard practice (curriculum) never got harder, because my "success" rule said the ball must go 1 to 15 m. The kick went 28 m, so every good kick counted as a failure. Oops.

So: success now = ball goes at least 1 m and the robot doesn't fall. Then the practice started getting harder like it should.

Step 4 · Kick v2 in the real match server

Falls 12 out of 12 times. Perfect in practice, useless in the stadium.

Why: the practice simulator secretly slows every joint down to its speed limit after each step. Like a safety rope on your arm. The real server has no rope. So the robot learned to swing as hard as possible, and in the real server the swing throws it over.

How I know: I added only that "rope" to the server and falls went from 12 to 1. The ball was also a bit different (weight, bounce).

Step 5 · Kick v4: practise without the rope

In the server: 0 falls out of 12. But with the real match delay, 12 falls again.

Why: in a real match your command arrives 20 milliseconds late. It had never practised with that delay.

Step 6 · Kick v5: practise with the delay too

In the server with the match delay: 11 out of 12 kicks, 1 fall, about 20 m, almost straight at the target.

Lesson: longer practice isn't always better. v4 got worse when it trained longer, so I tested every saved version in the server.

Still broken · Using the robot's own camera

It kicks, then falls (10 out of 12).

Why: in practice it always knew exactly where the ball was. The real camera is narrow and noisy, and the ball disappears from view right after the kick. The robot gets confused and loses balance.

Next: practise with a fake camera that's just as bad, so it learns to cope.

Get-up: what I tried, and why it fails

Step 1 · The robot fell through the floor

Why: to run faster, the walking setup only lets the feet touch the floor. A fallen robot touches the floor with its back and arms, so it sank.

So: gave the whole body back its collision shape for get-up.

Step 2 · Only learned small wobbles

Recovers from a small tilt, never from lying down.

Why: the practice only gets harder after a streak of successes, and it only succeeded about half the time, so it stayed stuck on easy.

Step 3 · Stuck at kneeling

Almost upright: stands 64 out of 64. Lying down: 0 out of 64. It gets to kneeling and stops.

Why: kneeling already earns most of the points (head fairly high, body fairly upright). Getting from kneeling to standing needs a big, forceful push, and I give minus points for forceful moves. So it settles for kneeling. That's a local optimum.

Now: two runs on the GPU. A: always start lying down, so it practises the hard part every time. B: same, but with much smaller minus points for force, so it's allowed to push hard first and get smooth later (the idea from the HumanUP paper).

What I learned

1. Perfect in practice can mean useless in the real game. Test in the real server early.

2. To find the real cause, change one thing at a time.

3. Check your success rule. Mine quietly stopped the practice from getting harder.

4. Robots cheat if the points let them.
